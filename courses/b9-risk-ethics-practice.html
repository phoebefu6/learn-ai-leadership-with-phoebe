Risk & ethics in practice: you are the last line of defense

When AI touches a decision about one of your people, you are the last line of defense - for their data, and for whether the call is fair. This session is not theory. You will learn the manager's risk map, the hard lines in daily work, and how to catch bias before it ships, then walk out with a people-data and bias checklist your whole team can use. The two rails run loud through all of it: AI advises, you decide and own it, and you protect your people.

Part 0

The hard frame

Here is the uncomfortable truth of this session. Your people cannot see what you paste into a chatbot, and they cannot see the bias in a recommendation that sounds confident and reasonable. That makes you - not the tool, not IT, not HR - the last line of defense for your team's data and for fairness in the decisions that shape their careers. AI will hand you a fluent, self-assured answer about a person; whether it is private and fair is on you. This session turns that responsibility into a checklist you can actually run.

Part 1 · covers the people-data + responsible-AI canon

The manager's risk map 7 min live

Four risks sit on a manager's desk, and they are different from the ones the whole company worries about, because yours are about the people you lead. Know all four by name, because you cannot defend against a risk you cannot see.

LiveThe four risks on your desk4 min▶

Each risk is quiet until it is not. Learn to name them so you catch them in the moment, not in the incident review.

  • People-data privacy. Employee PII, performance notes, and HR records are protected data - GDPR, PDPA, and employment law apply to your team exactly as they do to customers. A name plus a performance note in a consumer tool is a leak.
  • Bias in people decisions. Hiring, promotion, performance - AI can take a biased pattern and hand it back as a confident, well-worded recommendation. It launders the bias into something that looks objective.
  • Disclosure. When AI materially affects a person - a screening step, a ranking - they generally deserve to know. Silence erodes trust fast when it comes out later.
  • Shadow-AI. Unapproved tools your team quietly adopts. Convenient, invisible, and outside every guardrail - which is exactly what makes it dangerous.
Rail two, said plainly If a real person's name or record is in what you are about to paste, and they would not be fine seeing where it went, stop. Anonymize, or use only an approved tool. You are the check that stands between your people's data and a tool that never forgets.
Self-studyWhy the manager is the last line2 min read▶

Policy, legal, and IT set the fences. But in the moment a manager is drafting a review, comparing two candidates, or talking through a struggling report, no one else is in the room. The paste happens on your keyboard; the recommendation lands in your judgment. That is why the defense has to live in your habits, not just in a policy document nobody reads mid-task.

  • You are closest to the data. The employee's real details pass through your hands first.
  • You are closest to the call. Fairness is decided where the decision is made - your desk.
  • You set the norm. Your team copies what you do with AI, not what the handbook says.
Part 2 · covers the people-data rail in daily work

The lines in daily work 5 min live

Rules only help if they survive contact with a busy Tuesday. So here are the three hard lines a manager must never cross - and, next to each, the safe move that gets you the same help without the risk. Read the right column as loudly as the left.

Paste an employee's name and record into a consumer toolNo real people-data in unapproved toolsStrip names and details, or use an HR-approved tool for anything identifiable
Let AI decide a rating, a promotion, or a PIPNo decision about a person is the AI's to makeUse AI to structure your thinking; you make and own the call

Notice every safe move keeps the help and removes the harm. You almost never have to choose between "use AI" and "protect my people" - you just have to take the anonymized, human-owned path.

Self-studyThe one-question test before you paste2 min read▶

You do not need to memorize the table. You need one reflex, run every single time before you hit paste on anything about a person:

Before you paste, ask "Is there a real person's data in here, and am I letting AI decide instead of advise?" If either answer is yes, stop - anonymize the data, and take back the decision. That reflex is rails one and two in a single breath.

Run it enough times and it stops being a rule and becomes instinct. That instinct is the whole point of this session.

Part 3 · covers bias in people decisions

Bias, concretely 4 min live

"Watch for bias" is useless as advice unless you know what it looks like and how to test for it. Here is bias in people decisions made concrete - how it shows up, how you check, and when you stop and escalate.

LiveHow bias shows up, and the check2 min▶

AI does not invent fairness; it reflects patterns in its inputs and hands them back with confidence. In people-work that shows up in specific, catchable ways.

  • Confident laundering. A biased leaning arrives as a crisp, reasonable-sounding recommendation. The polish is what makes it dangerous.
  • Uneven language. Warm words for some team members, clinical or harsh words for others doing similar work - watch the adjectives.
  • Proxy signals. Tenure, school, a name, a gap in dates standing in for a protected trait or an irrelevant one.
The two checks that catch most of it Consistency: would this recommendation hold for everyone on the team doing comparable work? Swap test: would it read the same if the name, gender, or background were different? If either wobbles, the bias is in the output - not in the person.
Self-studyWhen to escalate to HR or legal2 min read▶

You are the last line of defense, not the whole legal system. Part of doing this well is knowing when the call is bigger than you - and raising it early, not after the decision is made.

  • Escalate when a people decision could have legal or fairness consequences and you are not sure it is clean - hiring, firing, promotion, pay, a PIP, an accommodation.
  • Escalate when the swap test wobbles and you cannot explain the difference on the merits.
  • Escalate when a tool captured or moved employee data it should not have, even by accident.
Escalating is strength, not weakness Raising a fairness concern early protects your person, your team, and you. The manager who asks "is this clean?" looks far better in six months than the one who shipped a confident, biased call.
Demo 1 of 2

Audit a manager's AI use for risk ★ 13 min · everyone builds

The best way to sharpen your eye is to catch someone else's near-miss. Here is an anonymized scenario of a manager using AI on people-work. Your job: flag every privacy and bias risk, then fix it. This is exactly the review you will run on your own habits after class.

The scenario

Anonymized - all names and details are invented. A team lead is deciding who to put forward for one promotion slot. They open a free consumer chatbot, paste in two reports' full names, last three performance ratings, salary, and a line about one being "on maternity leave earlier this year", and ask: "Who is more promotable? Just tell me who to pick." The AI names one, calling them "more consistent and committed". The lead copies that line into the promotion note. No approval, no disclosure, no second look.

Read the scenario and list every risk you can find. Do not fix yet - just flag. Aim for at least five.

Privacy flags: real names, ratings, salary, and a maternity reference - all people-data - in an unapproved consumer tool. That is rail two broken outright.

Bias flags: the "committed" verdict leans on a protected circumstance (maternity leave); "just tell me who to pick" hands the decision to the AI - rail one broken.

Process flags: no bias check, no disclosure, and the AI's wording copied straight into an official note as if it were the manager's own judgment.

Now fix it. Use the prompt below to rebuild the same task the safe way - anonymized inputs, a structured comparison, an explicit bias check, and the decision left to the human.

What the manager did - do not copy thisHere are two people up for promotion: [real name], ratings [x], salary [x], on maternity leave earlier this year; and [real name], ratings [x]. Who is more promotable? Just tell me who to pick.
★ The safe rebuild - anonymized, structured, human-ownedI am comparing two team members for one promotion slot. I have removed all names and anything personal. Person A: [neutral, job-relevant evidence only]. Person B: [same]. Do NOT tell me who to pick. Instead: (1) lay out a fair comparison against the promotion criteria only; (2) flag anything in my evidence that could be bias or an irrelevant proxy; (3) tell me what job-relevant information is missing. I will make and own the decision.
The audit habit "Flag, then fix" is the move. Once a week, look back at how you used AI on people-work and run this same two-pass review on yourself. You will catch your own near-misses before they become incidents.
Demo 2 of 2

Write your team's people-data + bias checklist ★ 8 min · build your own

The artifact you take away: a one-page checklist any manager on your team can run in ten seconds. Three columns - green light, red line, always. You draft it with AI, then edit it hard, which is rail one in miniature.

Give the AI your context: what people-data you handle, which tools are approved (or "none yet"), and the two rails.

Ask for three short lists - Green light (safe: anonymized thinking, structured comparisons, drafting with your judgment), Red line (never: real people-data in consumer tools, letting AI decide a rating or promotion, skipping the bias check), and Always (anonymize, run the swap test, human owns the call, escalate when unsure).

Edit it hard. Add your real approved tools, your org's escalation path to HR and legal, and the specific people-decisions your team makes. The draft is 70%; your judgment is the 30% that matters.

Share it with every manager on your team. You will fold it into the team operating model in the finale, b10.

★ The checklist promptDraft a one-page "people-data + bias checklist" for the managers on my team. Context: we handle team performance and some HR data; approved tools are [list, or "none yet"]; escalation goes to [HR / legal contact]. Build it around two rails: 1) AI advises, the manager decides and owns it - never let AI decide a rating, promotion, or PIP. 2) Protect people-data - no employee names, performance, or HR data in consumer tools; anonymize to think; run a bias check (consistency + would it hold if the name changed) on every people decision. Format: three short lists - Green light, Red line, Always. Plain language, one page, so any manager can run it in ten seconds.
Real world

The checklist that stopped the quiet leak. One team's managers were each guessing where the line was - some pasting review notes into free tools, some too scared to use AI at all. A one-page green/red/always card, agreed in an afternoon, gave everyone the same reflex before touching people-work. Managers got faster on the thinking, and not one employee record left an approved tool again.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Finish both demos if you did not complete them live - especially the audit. Catching the invented manager's near-misses trains you to catch your own.
  • Run the "flag, then fix" audit on one real way you used AI on people-work recently. Anonymize before you write anything down.
  • Finalize your one-page people-data + bias checklist and share it with every manager on your team.
  • Confirm your escalation path: who in HR or legal do you raise a fairness or data concern to, and how fast?
  • Optional: pick one recent people recommendation and run the swap test on it. Notice whether it holds when the name changes.
