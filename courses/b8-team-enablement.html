Part 0

Your job is the team's capability, not your own

By now you can use AI on decisions, communication, planning, feedback, delegation, and meetings. This session flips the lens outward. A manager who is a brilliant AI user but leads a team that avoids it - or worse, uses it in secret with no guardrails - has failed at the part that scales. Enablement is the leadership move: model it, share what works, set the rails, and make good use visible so it spreads.

Part 1 · covers reskilling programs

The manager as enabler 6 min live

You do not have to be the team's best prompter. You have to build a team that uses AI well - and that is a different, more durable skill. It runs on a loop: you model good use, share prompts that work, set clear guardrails, notch quick wins, and capability compounds from there.

LiveModel, share, guardrail, celebrate3 min▶

Enablement is four repeatable behaviors, not a one-off training day. Do them consistently and the team's capability grows on its own.

  • Model good use. Show your work. When AI helped you draft or stress-test something, say so, and show the prompt. People copy what their manager visibly does.
  • Share prompts. A prompt that worked for you works for the team. Put it somewhere shared so nobody reinvents it.
  • Set guardrails. The two rails from b1 - AI advises, you decide and own it; protect people-data - are the guardrails. Make them explicit so people feel safe to try.
  • Celebrate learning. Praise the person who found a useful prompt or caught an AI mistake. What gets celebrated gets repeated.
★ Try it now (any chat AI) - a modeling habitI want to model good AI use for my team. Give me three simple, repeatable ways to show my team how I use AI in my own work each week - without turning it into extra process. Keep it lightweight and specific to a team lead.
Real world

The manager who shared her screen. A team lead started ending updates with "here is the prompt I used for this." Within a month, three people on her team had adapted her prompts and shared their own back. She was not the best prompter in the room by then - and that was exactly the point. Her job was the team's capability, not her own.

Self-studyYou are the enabler, not the expert2 min read▶

Two traps catch managers here. The first is trying to be the team's AI expert - you will not scale, and you do not need to. The second is doing nothing and hoping people figure it out - they will, but in the shadows, with no guardrails. The enabler's job sits in the middle: create the conditions for the team to get good, and get out of the way.

Rail one applies to you as a leader too: AI advises your team, each person still owns their own judgment and output. Enablement is about capability with accountability, never handing the thinking to the tool.

Building capability 5 min live

Capability is built with a handful of concrete moves: a shared prompt library, safe places to practice, a quick-win use case for each role, the two rails as guardrails, and patient coaching for the skeptics. Each one has an effect you can see.

LiveThe moves that build a capable team3 min▶

Pick the moves that fit your team and run them. None of them require you to be an expert - they require you to be deliberate.

MoveEffect

One quick win per role The fastest way to convert a team is a use case tied to each person's real work - the drafting task they hate, the synthesis that eats their Friday. Land one visible win per role and adoption stops being a memo and becomes a habit.
Self-studyGuardrails make people braver, not smaller2 min read▶

Managers sometimes fear that guardrails will discourage use. The opposite is true - clear lines are what let people try. When the team knows exactly what is safe (anonymized thinking, drafting, synthesis) and what is off-limits (customer or people-data in consumer tools, letting AI make the call), they experiment inside the safe zone instead of freezing at the edge.

★ Coach-the-skeptic promptOne person on my team is skeptical of AI and reluctant to try it. Help me find one low-stakes, high-relevance quick win for their role [describe the role], and suggest how I could introduce it without making them feel pushed or judged.
Part 3 · covers reskilling programs

Sustaining it 4 min live

A launch is easy; a habit is hard. Enablement sticks when good AI use is visible and rewarded, when there is a regular cadence for sharing what works, and when you close off the shadow-AI risk - people using unapproved tools because no sanctioned path exists.

LiveVisible, cadenced, and no shadow AI3 min▶

Three things keep enablement alive past the first month.

  • Make good use visible and rewarded. Call out the person who found a clever prompt or caught an AI error. Recognition is the cheapest, strongest driver of repeat behavior.
  • Keep a cadence. A recurring five-minute "what worked with AI this week" slot keeps the library growing and normalizes sharing. Small and regular beats a big one-off.
  • Close the shadow-AI gap. When people use unapproved tools, it is almost always because there is no sanctioned path. Banning drives it underground; giving them an approved tool and clear guardrails brings it into the light.
Shadow AI is a signal, not a crime If your team is quietly pasting work into random tools, that is demand with no supply. The fix is a sanctioned path plus the two rails, not a crackdown. People want to do good work - give them a safe way to.
Self-studyThe two rails, at team scale2 min read▶

Everything you enable rides on the same two rails, now applied across a team.

  • AI advises, each person decides and owns it. Enablement grows capability, not dependence. Every team member still owns their judgment and output - AI is the assistant, never the author of record.
  • Protect people-data and customer data. The guardrails you publish are what make a sanctioned path safe. Clear lines let people experiment freely inside them.

You will pull all of this together in b9 (risk and ethics in practice) and the finale b10, where the team's operating model and cadence become the way you actually run.

Build a starter team prompt library ★ 13 min · everyone builds

Five role-relevant prompts, each with a guardrail, in one shared place. This is the single most useful artifact you can hand a team - the best use, ready to copy, with the lines drawn.

List the roles on your team and the one task each does most that AI could help with - drafting, synthesis, planning, first-pass analysis.

Run the library prompt below. Ask for five prompts, one per role or task, each written to be copy-pasteable by someone who is not a prompt expert.

Add a guardrail to each. Every prompt gets a one-line note - what not to put in it (people-data, customer data) and the reminder that the human owns the output. That is rail two, baked in.

Edit for your team's real work. Swap the generic tasks for the actual ones your people do. The AI draft is the skeleton; your knowledge of the team is the value.

Put it somewhere shared and tell the team it exists. A library nobody knows about is not a library.

★ The prompt-library promptHelp me build a starter prompt library for my team. Here are the roles and their most common task: [list, e.g. "PM - write a status update; analyst - summarize findings; designer - draft a brief"]. For each, give me: - A clear, copy-pasteable prompt a non-expert could use right away. - A one-line guardrail: what NOT to put in it (people-data, customer data) and a reminder that the human owns and checks the output. Give me 5 total. Keep them plain and practical, not clever.
Real world

Five prompts that changed a team's week. A manager built a five-prompt library - one for status updates, one for meeting synthesis, one for first-draft analysis, one for briefs, one for tough emails - each with a guardrail line. She shared it in the team channel. Within two weeks it had grown to eleven prompts, all contributed by the team. The library was the seed; the team grew it.

Draft a 30-day team enablement plan ★ 8 min · build your own

A simple month-long plan across five moves: how you will model good use, share the library, land quick wins per role, set the guardrails, and keep a cadence going. Concrete enough to start Monday.

Set the frame: your team size, the tools you have (or want) approved, and where you are today - from "nobody uses it" to "everyone secretly does."

Run the plan prompt below. Ask for a 30-day plan organized by the five moves: model, share, quick wins, guardrails, cadence.

Make it real. Pick actual dates, the real quick win per role, and the recurring slot where the team shares what worked.

Add your shadow-AI answer. Name the sanctioned tool and path so people never need to go around you.

Keep it to one page. A plan you will actually run beats a thorough one you will not.

★ The 30-day enablement plan promptDraft a one-page 30-day AI enablement plan for my team. Context: [team size], approved tools are [list, or "none yet"], and right now [describe where you are - e.g. "a few people use AI, most are unsure, no shared guardrails"]. Organize it around five moves: 1) Model - how I visibly use AI in my own work. 2) Share - stand up and grow a shared prompt library. 3) Quick wins - one relevant use case per role in week one. 4) Guardrails - the two rails (AI advises + I own it; protect people-data) made explicit. 5) Cadence - a recurring slot to share what worked and keep it alive. Give concrete weekly actions and address shadow AI - how a sanctioned path removes the reason people use unapproved tools. Keep it to one page.
Why 30 days A month is long enough to build a habit and short enough to actually finish. Enablement is not a launch event; it is a loop you keep running. The plan just gets the flywheel turning - after that, cadence carries it.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Finish your five-prompt library, each with a guardrail line, and put it somewhere the team can see and add to.
  • Finalise your 30-day plan and pick the first quick win - one role, one real task, this week.
  • Model it once: share a prompt you used, out loud, with your team. Watch what happens.
  • Name your sanctioned path. If there is no approved tool yet, that is your shadow-AI risk - raise it (the leader session a5 on adoption helps).
  • Optional: set up the recurring "what worked with AI this week" slot so the library keeps growing without you pushing it.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What is the manager's real job in AI enablement?

Enablement scales; being the resident expert does not. The manager creates the conditions - modeling, a shared library, clear guardrails, recognition - and lets the team's capability grow.

2 · What is the fastest way to convert a skeptical team to AI?

A visible win on a task someone actually cares about beats mandates and memos. Guardrails make people brave enough to try; a real quick win makes them stay.

3 · Your team is using unapproved AI tools in secret. What does that signal?

Shadow AI is a symptom of no sanctioned path, not a discipline problem. Banning drives it underground; an approved tool plus the two rails brings it into the light.
