Leading change & adoption: the pilot is the easy part

Buying the tool was never the hard part. Getting people to change how they actually work - and proving it moved the business - is where most AI efforts quietly stall. This session is about leading that change: meeting resistance with the why and support instead of mandates, and measuring real outcomes instead of counting logins. By the end you have an adoption plan for one workflow, with an honest ROI baseline.

Part 0

Why adoption is the whole game

A leader can approve every tool, sign every licence, and still see nothing change - because the tool was never the point. The point is people working differently, and people do not change how they work because a memo told them to. They change when they understand the why, see a quick win, feel supported through the awkward middle, and trust that this is not a headcount trick. This session gives you the leadership moves to make that happen, and the honest measures to know whether it did.

Part 1 · covers change-management + adoption programs

Adoption is a change problem, not a tech problem 6 min live

Resistance to AI is usually rational, not stubborn. People are protecting their competence, their time, and sometimes their job. You do not overcome that with a mandate - you meet it with a clear why, a quick win they can feel, reskilling so they stay valuable, and real support through the messy middle. Do that and skeptics become pragmatists, and pragmatists become champions.

LiveResistance is rational - lead it, do not override it3 min▶

When a capable person resists a new tool, they are usually right about something. They are protecting a workflow that works, guarding time they cannot spare to relearn, or quietly worried this ends with fewer of them. A mandate treats all of that as noise. Leadership treats it as information and answers it directly.

  • Answer the why first. Why this, why now, and what is in it for the person doing the work - not just for the P&L. Skip this and you get compliance, not adoption.
  • Engineer a quick win. One task that is visibly faster or better in week one does more than any slide. People believe what they feel.
  • Reskill, do not just deploy. Support through the awkward middle - training, time, a person to ask - is the difference between a tool people use and one they route around.
Questions to ask your team "Where is the real resistance, and what is it protecting?" · "What is the honest why for the person doing this work, not just for the business?" · "What quick win could we deliver in the first two weeks?"
Self-studyWhy the pilots fail - and it is not the AI3 min read▶

The uncomfortable headline from the field: most GenAI pilots show no measurable P&L impact. The instinct is to blame the model. The evidence points elsewhere - the pilots that failed skipped the change work. The tool got switched on; the way people worked never changed; so nothing downstream moved.

  • The tool was adopted, the workflow was not. Buying access is not the same as redesigning how the work gets done around it. Value lives in the workflow change, not the licence.
  • Success got declared too early. A demo that impressed the room is not a habit that survived a busy Tuesday. Pilots that stopped at the demo never touched the P&L.
  • Nobody owned adoption. When adoption is everyone's job, it is no one's. The pilots that stuck had a named owner for the change, not just the tech.
Real world

The pilot everyone praised and no one used. A team stood up an impressive AI pilot, showed it off, and moved on. Six months later the P&L had not moved a cent - because the actual workflows never changed and no one was accountable for making them. The AI worked fine. The change leadership never happened.

Questions to ask your team "Did the workflow actually change, or did we just add a tool on top?" · "Who owns adoption for this - by name?" · "Are we calling the demo a success too early?"
Part 2 · covers AI adoption ROI practice

Measuring real ROI, not activity theater 5 min live

Tool logins, prompts sent, and seats activated feel like progress and prove nothing. They are activity theater - motion mistaken for outcome. Real ROI is measured in business outcomes, capacity redeployed to higher-value work, and cycle-time cut. The discipline is simple: take a baseline before, and put a real decision gate after.

Honest metric (measures outcomes)Vanity metric (activity theater)
Cycle-time cut on a real workflow (days to hours)Number of logins or active seats
Capacity redeployed to higher-value workPrompts sent or messages generated
Business outcome moved (quality, revenue, cost, satisfaction)Tools rolled out or licences purchased
Error or rework rate down on the taskHours of training delivered
Baseline-vs-after delta on a named metric"People love it" with no number behind it
LiveBaseline before, decision gate after3 min▶

You cannot prove impact you never measured before you started. The single most valuable thing a leader does at the front of a pilot is capture the baseline - how long this took, how much it cost, how good it was - before AI touches it. Then you set a decision gate: at a fixed date, the pilot has to show a real delta on that metric or it does not scale.

  • Baseline first, always. Without a before, every after is a story. Measure the current cycle-time, cost, and quality of the target workflow before you change anything.
  • Pick outcome metrics, not activity metrics. Redeployed capacity and cycle-time answer "did the business get better?" Logins answer "did we buy a tool?"
  • Put a real gate after. A named date and a threshold: hit the delta and scale, miss it and stop or rethink. A gate you never enforce is not a gate.
Questions to ask your team "What is our baseline number, captured before we start?" · "Which outcome - not activity - are we betting this moves?" · "What is the date and the threshold at our decision gate?"
Self-studyRedeployed capacity is the honest win2 min read▶

The most defensible ROI story is rarely headcount cut - it is capacity redeployed. When AI takes eight hours of low-value work off a team, the win is what those eight hours now do: the analysis that never got done, the customer that got more attention, the backlog that finally moved. Name where the freed capacity went, and the value becomes real and honest instead of a vague "efficiency" claim.

  • Follow the freed time. Redeployed to higher-value work is a win you can point at; "saved time" that vanishes into more meetings is not.
  • Be honest about the people story. If the plan is augment-not-replace, the redeployment is the proof. If it is not, say so plainly - do not launder it as efficiency.
  • Cycle-time is the cleanest number. Days-to-hours on a real workflow is hard to fake and easy for a board to understand.
Questions to ask your team "Where exactly did the freed capacity go?" · "Can we point to the higher-value work it enabled?" · "Are we telling an honest augment story, or dressing up a cut?"
Part 3 · the move that makes adoption stick

From pilot to habit 4 min live

A pilot ends; a habit persists. The path between them is deliberate: pick a measurable pilot, support the humans through the change, scale what genuinely works, and retire what does not - honestly and quickly. Reskilling is the enabler that turns a one-off pilot into how the team simply works now.

LivePick measurable, support humans, scale, retire2 min▶

The leaders who make AI stick run a tight loop. They choose one workflow where success can be measured, not a vague "explore AI". They invest in the people through the awkward middle. They scale only what cleared the ROI gate, and they kill what did not - fast, and without ego - so the team keeps trusting the process.

  • Pick a measurable pilot. One workflow, one baseline, one outcome metric. Measurable beats ambitious.
  • Support the humans through it. Change is uncomfortable in the middle. Training, time, and a champion to ask are what carry people across.
  • Scale what works, retire what does not. Cleared the gate - scale it deliberately. Missed it - retire it honestly. Zombie pilots that never die drain trust and budget.
Questions to ask your team "Is this pilot actually measurable, or just interesting?" · "What support do the humans need through the middle?" · "What have we kept alive that we should have retired?"
Self-studyReskilling is the enabler, not the afterthought2 min read▶

Reskilling is often treated as a nice-to-have you get to later. It is actually the mechanism that turns adoption from a mandate into a habit. People adopt what they feel competent with. When you invest in their skill - and visibly protect their value - resistance drops, because the change stops feeling like a threat and starts feeling like an upgrade to them.

  • Skill builds willingness. Competence removes fear. The most reliable way to reduce resistance is to make people genuinely good at the new way of working.
  • Reskilling is the augment promise, kept. "We are augmenting, not replacing" is only credible if you invest in people's new skills. The training is the proof of the promise.
  • Champions come from here. The reskilled early adopters become the people who pull the rest of the team along - worth more than any top-down push.
Questions to ask your team "Are we treating reskilling as core or as an afterthought?" · "Does our training make people feel more valuable, or more replaceable?" · "Who are our champions, and are we growing them on purpose?"
Your turn

Draft an adoption plan for one workflow ◐ 20 min · leaders

Pick one real workflow your team could put AI into. Draft the adoption plan a leader would stand behind: the why, the quick win, the support, the baseline, and the honest success metric. This is the artifact you leave with.

  • Name the workflow and the why. One workflow, and the honest why for the person doing it - not just the business case. If you cannot state the why for the human, that is your first gap.
  • Capture the baseline before. How long does this take, cost, and how good is it today? Write the number down before anything changes.
  • Choose one honest success metric. An outcome or capacity or cycle-time metric - not logins. Set the decision gate: the date and the threshold to scale.
  • Plan the support and reskilling. What do the humans need through the middle - training, time, a champion? Write the support in, not as an afterthought.
  • Decide what "retire" looks like. If the gate is missed, what happens - and who has the authority to call it? Agree it now, while it is easy.
Questions to ask your team "Would this plan survive a busy Tuesday, or only a demo?" · "Is the baseline real and captured before we start?" · "If this misses the gate, are we honest enough to retire it?"
