Part 0

Where meetings go to be wasted

A manager spends hours in meetings, and the value leaks out the moment they end: who agreed to what, what we actually decided, who owns the next step. AI is unusually good here - it listens, summarizes, and pulls out decisions and actions while you stay present and run the conversation. But the same tools record real people saying real things, so this session pairs the technique with the consent-and-governance habit that has to come with it.

Part 1 · covers management practice - meetings

Meetings into decisions and actions 6 min live

A notetaker or assistant can turn an hour of talk into a summary, a list of decisions, and owned action items in seconds - the record-keeping that used to eat your attention or never got done. That frees you to do the human part: run the room, read it, and drive to real decisions. The tool captures; you still lead and own the follow-through.

LiveSummary, decisions, actions - the three outputs3 min▶

A meeting has three useful outputs, and AI can draft all three from a transcript or a set of notes. Your job is to make the room produce real decisions in the first place, then verify what the AI pulled out.

  • The summary - what was discussed, in a few lines someone who missed it can read. AI does this well; skim it for accuracy.
  • The decisions - what we actually agreed. If the AI cannot find a clear decision, that is a signal the meeting did not make one.
  • The owned actions - each next step with a name and a rough when. "Someone should" is not an action; a named owner is.
★ Try it now (any chat AI) - three outputs from notesHere are my rough notes from a team meeting: [paste anonymized notes]. Give me three sections: a 4-line summary, a list of decisions we made, and action items - each with an owner and a suggested due date. Flag anything that sounds like an open question rather than a decision.
Real world

The weekly that finally moved. A team's staff meeting had run for a year with no written record - the same topics resurfaced every week. The manager started dropping the notetaker transcript into an assistant for a summary, decisions, and owned actions, sent within the hour. Nothing about the discussion changed; what changed was that decisions now stuck and owners knew they were owners.

Self-studyYou still own the follow-through2 min read▶

The AI draft is a starting point, not the record. Two things stay yours.

  • Verify before you send. Notetakers mishear names, attribute the wrong person, and occasionally invent a decision that was only floated. Read it once against your memory.
  • Chase the actions. A list of owned actions only matters if you follow up. The tool remembers; accountability is a human habit.

Rail one in this session: AI captures and drafts, the manager runs the room and owns the follow-up. The neat summary is worthless if nobody does the next thing.

Part 2 · covers management practice - facilitation

Prep and facilitate better 5 min live

The best way to fix a meeting is upstream. AI drafts the agenda, turns a pile of pre-reads into a short brief, and hands you facilitation prompts, so the time in the room goes to decisions instead of status updates everyone could have read. Synthesize the inputs before people arrive.

LiveAgendas, pre-reads, and facilitation prompts3 min▶

AI helps at every stage of the meeting, not just the notes at the end. Point it at the prep.

Set the agendaDraft a timed agenda from your goal - decisions to make, not topics to cover

Synthesize inputsPull themes and open questions out of survey answers or status notes beforehand

Turn status into decisions If your agenda is a list of updates, send them as a written pre-read instead and reclaim the meeting for the two or three real decisions. Ask AI: "which of these agenda items could be an async update rather than a live discussion?"
Self-studySynthesize before, decide during2 min read▶

The move that saves the most time is synthesis before the meeting. If you have collected input - a survey, a round of written comments, three teams' status - drop it into an assistant and ask for the themes, the disagreements, and the decisions that need making. Now the room debates a tight set of real questions instead of hearing five people read their update aloud.

★ Pre-meeting synthesis promptHere is input from the team ahead of our planning meeting: [paste anonymized comments]. Group it into 3-4 themes, list the points of disagreement, and give me the 3 decisions we most need to make in the room. Suggest one framing question for each decision.
Part 3 · covers people-data + HR/employment canon

The consent and governance note 4 min live

A notetaker is a recording device with a memory. It captures colleagues' voices, opinions, and sometimes sensitive people-data, and it stores a transcript somewhere. That is people-data, and the manager owns how it is handled - consent, storage, and what ends up in the summary you forward.

LiveConsent, storage, and what goes in the summary3 min▶

Before you let a notetaker into a meeting, three things are on you.

  • Consent to record. Tell people a notetaker is running, check your org's policy and local law, and give anyone a way to opt out. A recording nobody agreed to is a problem, not a convenience.
  • Where it lives. Know where the transcript and any recording are stored, who can access them, and how long they are kept. If you cannot answer that, do not run the tool on a sensitive conversation.
  • What you send. The auto-summary can contain a stray sensitive comment or an off-hand remark about a person. Read it and strip anything that should not go to the whole distribution before you forward.
Never on the sensitive ones One-to-ones, performance conversations, and anything with HR or legal weight should not go through a consumer notetaker at all. The rail from session b1 holds: real people-data lives in approved tools, or the conversation stays off the record.
Self-studyThe two rails, in meetings2 min read▶

Both rails show up in this session, side by side.

  • AI advises, you decide and own it. The notetaker drafts the record and the assistant drafts the actions - you verify them, run the room, and chase the follow-through.
  • Protect people-data. A notetaker captures colleagues' words. Consent, storage, and what leaves the room are the manager's responsibility, and sensitive conversations stay out of consumer tools.

Say it plainly to your team once: "we use a notetaker, here is what it records and where it goes, and you can ask me to turn it off." That single sentence is most of the governance.

Demo 1 of 2

Transcript to summary, decisions, and owned actions ★ 13 min · everyone builds

The core move of the session. Take a meeting transcript - anonymized or synthetic - and turn it into a clean summary, a list of decisions, and owned action items, then verify what the AI produced against what you know happened.

Get a transcript. Use a synthetic one (write four short lines of a fake planning meeting) or a real one with every name replaced by a role - "the PM", "the designer" (rail two).

Run the summary-to-actions prompt below. Ask for three sections: a short summary, the decisions made, and action items with an owner and a due date.

Verify. Read each decision - is it something you actually agreed, or something the AI inferred? Check every action has a real owner. This is rail one: you own the record.

Fix the gaps. Where the AI found "an open question" instead of a decision, that is a real meeting problem to close, not a summary to patch over.

Draft the follow-up message. Ask the AI to turn the verified output into a short note you would actually send to the team.

★ The summary-to-actions promptHere is a meeting transcript. Names have been replaced with roles. [paste anonymized / synthetic transcript] Give me three sections: 1) Summary - 4 to 5 lines, readable by someone who missed it. 2) Decisions - what we actually agreed. If something was only floated, put it under "open questions" instead of decisions. 3) Action items - each with an owner (by role) and a suggested due date. Then draft a short follow-up message I could send to the team with the decisions and actions. Flag anything you were unsure about so I can verify it.
Why verify matters here A confident summary that quietly invents a decision is worse than no summary - people act on it. Read the decisions section against your own memory every time. The AI drafts fast; you are the one who signs off that it is true.
Demo 2 of 2

Build a reusable meeting kit ★ 8 min · build your own

Three saved prompts you will reuse every week: an agenda template, a pre-read synthesis prompt, and the summary-to-actions prompt from demo one. Build the kit once, run better meetings forever.

Ask AI for an agenda template built around decisions - a timed structure with a stated goal, the decisions to make, and a slot for owned actions at the end.

Save the pre-read synthesis prompt from Part 2 - the one that boils collected input into themes, disagreements, and the decisions to make in the room.

Save the summary-to-actions prompt from demo one as the third tool in the kit.

Add your consent line - the one sentence you say when a notetaker is running - so governance travels with the kit.

Keep the three prompts plus the consent line somewhere you will find them - a note, a doc, a saved prompt. Reuse beats re-inventing every Monday.

★ The meeting-kit promptHelp me build a reusable meeting kit I can use every week. Give me: 1) A timed agenda template built around decisions, not status - with a goal line, the decisions to make, and an action-items slot at the end. 2) A short pre-read synthesis prompt I can paste collected input into. 3) A summary-to-actions prompt I run on the transcript afterward. Keep each one plain and copy-pasteable. Also suggest a single sentence I can say at the top of a meeting to tell people a notetaker is recording and that they can opt out.
Real world

The kit that shortened every meeting. A team lead saved three prompts and a consent line into one note. Agendas got tighter because they were built around decisions; pre-reads meant status stopped eating the room; summaries went out within the hour with owners named. The meetings themselves got 15 minutes shorter, and people stopped asking "wait, what did we decide?"

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Run the summary-to-actions prompt on one real (anonymized) meeting this week. Verify the decisions against your memory before you send anything.
  • Finish your meeting kit - the three saved prompts plus your consent line - and use the agenda template on your next meeting.
  • Do the synthesis move once: collect written input before a meeting and boil it down to themes and decisions, so the room debates instead of updates.
  • Check where your notetaker stores transcripts and who can see them. If you cannot answer, find out before you record another sensitive conversation.
  • Optional: pick one recurring meeting and ask AI which agenda items could become an async update instead.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What are the three useful outputs AI can pull from a meeting?

A meeting is worth capturing as a summary, the decisions actually agreed, and action items each with an owner. "Someone should" is not an action - a named owner is.

2 · What is the best way to make a meeting go to decisions, not status?

Fix the meeting upstream: use AI to draft a decisions-based agenda and boil collected input into themes and the decisions to make, so status moves to a pre-read.

3 · A notetaker is running in your team meeting. What is on you as the manager?

Notetakers capture people-data. Consent to record, storage and access, and stripping sensitive detail from the summary are the manager's to govern - and sensitive conversations stay out of consumer tools.
