Planning & prioritization: AI drafts the plan, you own the tradeoffs

A goal, a pile of inputs, and not enough time is the manager's default state. AI can turn a goal into OKRs, a roadmap, and a scored backlog, and boil a mess of survey answers and asks into clear themes - fast. But a plan is a set of tradeoff calls, and those are the strategy. Tonight you draft OKRs from a real goal and synthesize a pile into a prioritized shortlist, then make the cuts yourself.

Part 0

Where AI helps a manager plan

Planning stalls in two places: staring at a goal with no idea how to break it down, and drowning in inputs with no idea what matters most. AI is strong at both - it drafts OKRs from an outcome, scores a backlog against a framework, and clusters a pile into themes in seconds. That gets you a plan to react to. The react is the whole job: you cut what does not matter, own the tradeoffs, and keep the goals few and honest.

Part 1 · covers OKR / planning canon

From goal to plan 5 min live

A plan is a chain: a goal becomes objectives and key results, key results become priorities, priorities become a roadmap and a backlog. AI can draft every link and synthesize scattered inputs into a clean starting point. What it cannot do is decide what matters most when everything looks important - that is strategy, and every tradeoff call along the chain is yours to own.

LiveAI drafts the links, you own the tradeoffs3 min▶

The trap with AI planning is that it makes everything look reasonable. Ask for a plan and you get a tidy, plausible, complete one - which feels like progress but hides the real work. The real work is choosing: fewer objectives, a sharper key result, the priority you will cut so the top one gets done. AI removes the drudgery of drafting; it must not remove the discipline of deciding.

  • Let it draft the chain. Goal to OKRs to priorities to roadmap - a first version in seconds beats a blank planning doc.
  • You make the cuts. A plan is defined by what it leaves out. AI will happily keep everything; your job is to say no.
  • Keep the goals few and honest. Three real objectives beat ten aspirational ones. If everything is a priority, nothing is.
★ Try it now (any chat AI) - goal to OKRsHere is a team goal for this quarter: [goal in one sentence]. Draft one clear objective and 3 measurable key results. Keep it to 3. Then tell me which key result is weakest and why, so I can sharpen or cut it.
Real world

The tidy plan that was quietly wrong. A manager asked AI for a quarterly plan and got a beautiful one - twelve initiatives, every box ticked. It went to the team as-is. Two weeks in, it was obvious nobody could do twelve things, and the two that actually mattered were buried in the middle. The plan looked complete and was undeliverable. The fix was not a better prompt - it was the manager doing the cut AI never will: three priorities, the rest parked. A tidy plan is not a right plan.

Self-studyWhat makes a key result actually good2 min read▶

AI drafts key results fast, but it defaults to vague ones. Sharpen every draft against these:

  • Measurable, not fuzzy. "Improve onboarding" is a wish. "Cut new-hire ramp from 8 weeks to 5" is a key result.
  • An outcome, not a task. "Ship the new flow" is activity. "Lift activation to 40%" is the result you actually want.
  • Honest, not sandbagged and not fantasy. A key result you will definitely hit was too easy; one you have no path to is theatre.
  • Few. Three per objective, max. More is a signal you have not chosen yet.

Have AI draft, then run every key result through this list yourself. That edit is where a generic plan becomes yours.

Part 2 · covers OKR / planning canon - prioritization

Prioritization with AI 5 min live

Prioritization frameworks are just structured prompts, and AI runs them well. Hand it impact/effort or a RICE-style scoring and it will draft the scores and cluster a pile of inputs - survey answers, notes, stakeholder asks - into a handful of themes. It does the sorting. You make the tradeoff, because a score is an input to judgment, not a substitute for it.

LiveFrameworks as prompts, the tradeoff stays yours3 min▶

A scoring framework does one useful thing: it forces a consistent comparison so the loudest request does not automatically win. AI is great at applying one evenly across a long list. But the score is only as good as the estimates behind it, and the final ranking often ignores something a number cannot hold - a dependency, a relationship, a strategic bet. So you use the score to think, then you decide.

  • Impact / effort - fastest sort. AI drafts both axes; you sanity-check the effort estimates, which are usually optimistic.
  • RICE-style (reach, impact, confidence, effort) - more rigor for a big list; AI computes, you challenge the confidence scores.
  • Synthesize, then theme - give AI the pile and ask for the 3 to 5 themes underneath it, with the strongest evidence for each.
The score is an input, not the answer Let AI rank the list, then look at the top and the cut line and ask: does this ranking feel right, and what is it missing? The number starts the conversation; your judgment ends it.

Part 3 · the discipline that keeps a plan honest

Keep it real 4 min live

AI produces plausible plans faster than you can check them, and plausible is exactly the danger. A confident roadmap can rest on assumptions nobody verified, targets nobody has a path to, and a scope no team could deliver. The habit that keeps you safe is boring and non-negotiable: surface the assumptions, verify the ones that matter, and never confuse a tidy plan for a right one.

LiveVerify assumptions, keep goals few and honest2 min▶

Every plan rides on assumptions - about capacity, about what will work, about what leadership will fund. AI buries them inside confident prose. Your move is to pull them into the light and pressure-test the load-bearing ones before you commit a team to the plan.

  • Ask for the assumptions. "What is this plan assuming is true?" - then verify the two or three that would break it if wrong.
  • Keep goals few and honest. Cut aspirational padding. A plan you can actually deliver beats an impressive one you cannot.
  • Tidy is not right. Polish is not evidence. The plan that looks best on the slide is not automatically the one that works.
Two questions before you commit "What is this plan assuming, and which assumption would sink it if it is wrong?" and "Can my team actually deliver this?" If either answer is shaky, the plan is not ready, however tidy it looks.
Demo 1 of 2

Draft OKRs from a real team goal ★ 13 min · everyone builds

Take one real goal for your team and turn it into a sharp set of OKRs with AI - an objective and measurable key results - then do the part that makes it yours: sharpen the weak ones and cut until only three remain.

Write your goal in one plain sentence - the outcome you actually want this quarter, not the activity.

Draft pass: ask AI for one objective and three measurable key results, and to name which key result is weakest.

Sharpen pass: fix every fuzzy key result - make it an outcome with a number, not a task. Kill "improve", "enhance", "support".

Honesty pass: for each target ask "do we have a path to this, and is it a real stretch?" Adjust anything sandbagged or fantasy.

Cut pass: hold it to three. If you kept more, you have not chosen yet. The cut is the strategy - own it out loud.

Prompt A - accept the tidy plan (do not)Give me a full quarterly plan for my team with all the OKRs and initiatives we should run.
★ Prompt B - draft, then you sharpen and cutTeam goal for this quarter: [one sentence, the outcome]. Draft ONE objective and exactly 3 measurable key results. For each key result make it an outcome with a number, not a task. Then: (1) tell me which key result is weakest and why; (2) list the assumptions this set is making; (3) flag any target you think is too easy or has no realistic path. I will sharpen and cut from there - keep it to three.
Real world

Three beat ten. A team lead drafted OKRs with AI and got a solid objective plus eight key results - all reasonable. Instead of shipping all eight, they used the "which is weakest" answer to cut down to three that actually mattered, and parked the rest. The quarter closed with all three hit. The version with eight would have ended with eight half-done. AI drafted the options; the manager owned the cut.

Demo 2 of 2

A pile of inputs into three themes + a shortlist ★ 8 min · build your own

Turn a mess of inputs - survey answers, notes, stakeholder asks - into three clear themes and a prioritized shortlist with an impact/effort call you can defend.

Gather your pile: retro notes, a survey, the list of asks. Remove anything that identifies a specific person if it is sensitive.

Ask AI to cluster it into three to five themes, with the strongest evidence for each - not just the loudest one.

Ask it to draft an impact/effort score for each candidate item and rank them, flagging the effort estimates it is least sure of.

Your call: look at the top and the cut line. Adjust the estimates you know are off, and pick the one item that ships first and what gets parked.

★ The synthesize-and-prioritize promptHere is a pile of inputs [paste survey answers / notes / asks]. 1) Cluster it into 3-5 themes. For each, give the strongest evidence - not just the most frequent, the most important. 2) List the candidate actions. Draft an impact (1-5) and effort (1-5) score for each and rank them. 3) Flag the effort estimates you are least confident about so I can correct them. I will make the final tradeoff call on what ships first and what we park.
Own the cut line AI gives you a ranked list; the cut line - where "yes" becomes "not now" - is a judgment call. Draw it yourself, name what falls below it, and be able to say why. That line is the plan.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Finish both demos if you did not complete them live - especially the cut pass on the OKRs. Choosing three is the lesson.
  • Take one real goal and draft OKRs with AI, then sharpen and cut to three you would defend to your boss.
  • Run the synthesize prompt on a real pile of inputs and see if the top theme matches your gut - and where it does not.
  • On any plan you already have, ask AI to list its assumptions, then verify the one that would sink it if wrong.
  • Optional: keep both prompts handy. "Goal to OKRs" and "synthesize and prioritize" are weekly moves.
