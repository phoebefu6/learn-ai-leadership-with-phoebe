Part 0

The rails, stated loudly

Everything else in this course sharpens how you use AI. This session draws the hard lines that keep it safe and fair. Three rails run loud through every block: AI advises, the leader decides and owns it - accountability for an AI-influenced decision never transfers to the model. Don't outsource judgment or empathy - especially on people decisions. Protect your people's data - no employee PII in consumer AI, and stay alert to bias whenever AI touches hiring, promotion, or performance. GDPR, PDPA, and employment law apply to your team's data exactly as they do to customers'.

Part 1 · covers the responsible-AI leadership canon

The guardrails a leader owns 10 min live

Responsible AI is a stack, and a leader owns every layer of it. It rests on a foundation of people-data and consent, rises through bias checks and human accountability, and is capped by transparency and a clear policy. Read it bottom-up: without the foundation, nothing above it is safe.

LiveAccountability and bias - the two that bite4 min▶

Two guardrails cause the most harm when they slip. First, accountability: an AI-influenced decision is still your decision, and "the model recommended it" is not a defence to your team, a regulator, or yourself. Second, bias - and it is sharpest on people decisions. AI can launder a biased historical pattern into a confident, tidy recommendation about who to hire, promote, or manage out. A human owns every such call, with fairness front of mind.

  • Accountability stays human. The leader owns the outcome of any AI-influenced decision. Ownership does not move to the tool, ever.
  • Bias hits people decisions hardest. Hiring, promotion, and performance are exactly where a laundered pattern does real damage. Review them for unfair effects.
  • Confidence is not fairness. A clean-looking AI recommendation about a person can still be biased. Interrogate it before you act.
Questions to ask your team Where is AI touching a decision about a person on our team? Who is named as accountable for that call? How would we even notice if the recommendation were biased?
Self-studyTransparency, explainability, and people-data privacy3 min read▶

The other two guardrails are about honesty and protection. Transparency means people know when AI shaped something that affects them, and you can explain the basis of the decision in plain terms - "we decided because", not "the algorithm said so". Privacy means your people's data gets the same care as any customer's: no employee names, performance details, or HR information in consumer AI tools, full stop.

  • Disclose where it affects people. If AI influenced an outcome someone experiences, they should know. Hidden AI erodes the trust the whole culture rests on.
  • Explainability is a leadership duty. You must be able to explain a people decision without hiding behind the model. If you cannot explain it, do not act on it.
  • People-data is protected data. Employee PII, performance, and HR data go only in enterprise or HR-approved tools - never a consumer chatbot.
Questions to ask your team Where are we using AI without telling the people it affects? Could we explain each people decision in plain language? Is any employee data going into tools that are not approved for it?

Self-studyBias laundering - the trap to name2 min read▶

Bias laundering is the quiet danger: a model trained on biased history hands you a confident, neutral-looking recommendation, and the bias arrives wearing the costume of objectivity. "The data said so" feels rigorous, which is exactly what makes it dangerous on a people decision. The leader's job is to treat any AI recommendation about a person as a claim to be tested, not a verdict.

  • Objectivity is a costume. A tidy score can hide an unfair pattern. Ask what the model learned from, and who it might disadvantage.
  • Test it against fairness. Before acting, ask whether the recommendation would hold up if the person's group changed.
  • Keep a human in the loop. On hiring, promotion, and performance, a person reviews and owns the call - always.
Part 3 · mirrors manager session b9

The responsible-AI stack you need 6 min live

You do not need a legal department to run responsible AI - you need five practical pieces in place. This is the leader-level mirror of the manager toolkit in b9: put these up, review them on a cadence, and you have covered the ground that matters.

LiveThe five pieces every team needs3 min▶

None of these are heavy. Together they turn the guardrails from good intentions into something your team actually operates.

  • Acceptable-use policy. A one-page "how we use AI here" - what is fine, what is off-limits, which tools are approved for what data.
  • Human-in-the-loop on people decisions. No hiring, promotion, or performance call is made by AI alone. A named human decides and owns it.
  • Bias review. A simple check on any AI-influenced people decision for unfair patterns before it lands.
  • Disclosure. Tell people when AI shaped something that affects them, and be able to explain it plainly.
  • Audit + review cadence. A regular look-back - what did AI touch, did anything go wrong, what do we adjust.
Questions to ask your team Do we have a written acceptable-use policy people have actually read? Which people decisions still lack a named human owner? When did we last review AI use for bias or harm?
Self-studyWhy the cadence matters most2 min read▶

A policy written once and never revisited rots. AI changes, your tools change, and new uses creep in that nobody signed off on. The review cadence is what keeps the stack alive: a recurring, low-ceremony check that AI use still matches the rails. Without it, the other four pieces slowly drift out of date.

  • Set a rhythm, not a one-off. Quarterly is plenty for most teams - what is AI touching now, and is it still safe and fair?
  • Catch the drift. New tools and new uses appear between reviews. The cadence is where you notice and re-approve them.
  • Close the loop. Any near-miss or complaint feeds back into the policy so the same gap does not reopen.
Real world

The screening tool nobody owned. A team quietly adopted an AI resume-screener. It ran for months with no named owner, no bias check, and no disclosure - until a rejected candidate asked how the decision was made and no one could answer. The fix was not more technology. It was a named human owner, a bias review, and a disclosure line - the stack that should have been there first.

Your turn

Responsible-AI gap-scan ◐ 12 min · everyone

A one-page artifact you keep: an honest scan of your team against the five-piece stack, and the questions you will put to your own team and to any AI vendor. You do this as a leader - no tool required.

List every place AI currently touches a decision about a person on your team - hiring, promotion, performance, scheduling, anything.

For each, check the five pieces: is there a named human owner, a bias review, disclosure, an approved tool for the data, and a review cadence? Mark the gaps.

Pick the biggest gap and decide the one move to close it this quarter - usually naming an owner or adding a bias review.

Write your vendor questions: is the model tested for bias, can its decisions be explained, and whose data was it trained on and where does our data go.

Set your review date. A responsible-AI stack without a cadence is a document, not a safeguard. You own the follow-through.

Questions to ask your team - and your vendors To your team: where does AI touch a people decision, and who owns it? To a vendor: has the model been tested for bias? Can its decisions be explained in plain terms? Whose data was it trained on, and where does ours go?
